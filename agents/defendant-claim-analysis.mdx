## What it does

Defendant Claim Analysis builds a litigation profile of the other side. Name an opposing **party** (defendant or plaintiff) and/or opposing **counsel** in your query, and Lawdie mines their recent cases on CourtListener, reads the underlying filings, and synthesizes:

1. **Recurring argument patterns** — the legal theories, claims, and defenses they reach for again and again
2. **Signature tactics** — the authorities, procedural moves, and framing they favor
3. **Likely counterarguments** — the positions you should expect from them, and the weaknesses to exploit against those positions

The result is a strategic read on the opposition *before* you write your brief — grounded in what they've actually filed, not guesswork.

<Note>
  Defendant Claim Analysis is not a standalone agent. It runs automatically inside the [Legal Research](/agents/legal-research) and [Rule Statement & RERA](/agents/rule-statement-rera) agents whenever you name someone to profile. There's nothing separate to switch on.
</Note>

## How to use it

Work through **Kiwi AI** as you normally would with Legal Research or Rule Statement, and simply name the opposing party and/or counsel in your prompt. Phrases like *"profile opposing counsel…"*, *"research the defendant…"*, or *"who are we up against…"* trigger it.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Name the opposition in your query">
    Add the opposing party and/or attorney to any Legal Research or Rule Statement prompt — e.g. *"…and profile opposing counsel Jane Doe."* You can name a party, an attorney, or both.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Confirm who you mean, if asked">
    If the name matches more than one person or entity on CourtListener, Lawdie pauses and lists the candidates with a sample case for each. Reply with the full name or its number to continue.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Lawdie profiles them before searching case law">
    It pulls their recent dockets, reads the filings, and synthesizes the profile *first* — so the profile is ready by the time your case-law results come back.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The profile is woven into your answer">
    In Legal Research, the argument patterns and counterarguments are folded into the response. In Rule Statement & RERA, they appear as a dedicated **Opposing Counsel / Party Profile** section and are used to sharpen the Rule Application.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Using it with Legal Research

Pair it with a normal case-law search when you want the lay of the land plus a read on the other side in one pass. The profile comes back alongside your search results, with the opposition's likely counterarguments called out.

> *"Find recent Ninth Circuit cases on trade secret misappropriation under the DTSA, and profile opposing counsel Marcus Feldman."*

> *"Pull case law on personal jurisdiction over out-of-state manufacturers, and research the defendant Acme Industries — what arguments do they usually run?"*

> *"Research opposing counsel Jane Doe. What's her playbook, and what should I be ready for?"*

## Using it with Rule Statement & RERA

This is where it's sharpest. When you draft RE/RA paragraphs, the opposition profile feeds directly into the **Rule Application** — the agent hardens your affirmative argument against the counterarguments the other side is most likely to raise, and adds an *Opposing Counsel / Party Profile* section to the output.

> *"Draft RE and RA for a 12(b)(6) motion. Element: failure to plead scienter for securities fraud. Jurisdiction: SDNY. Client facts: complaint alleges only that our client 'must have known' the projections were false. Also profile plaintiff's counsel, Thomas Girardi, so I know what they'll push back with."*

> *"Generate RERA on the duty element in a premises-liability case in the 11th Circuit, and profile the defendant BigBox Retail — I want the RA to pre-empt their usual no-constructive-notice argument."*

## What you need for a good profile

The profile is only as good as what the opposition has publicly filed. Results are strongest when:

| Factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| The party/attorney has **recent federal cases** | The profile is built from CourtListener's RECAP (federal PACER) records |
| Their filings are **publicly readable** | Documents locked behind the PACER paywall can't be read, which thins the profile |
| The name is **specific** | Full names disambiguate faster; a common name may trigger the confirmation step |

<Note>
  Lawdie inspects up to the 10 most recent dockets and summarizes up to 12 filings per profile. A litigant with little federal filing history, or whose documents aren't publicly available, will produce a limited profile — Lawdie tells you when that happens rather than inventing detail.
</Note>

## Tips for better results

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Use full names">
    "Thomas Vincent Girardi" resolves cleanly; "T. Girardi" or a common surname alone is more likely to match multiple people and trigger the confirmation step. Middle names and initials still match.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Profile both the party and their counsel">
    They reveal different things — the party's profile surfaces the substantive positions they take, while counsel's profile surfaces litigation style and procedural tactics. Naming both gives you the fullest picture.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Say what you're up against">
    Pair the profile request with your own element and facts (as in the RERA examples). The more the agent knows about your case, the more targeted the counterarguments it surfaces.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Entity names work too">
    You can profile a company, not just an individual — e.g. "profile the defendant Johnson & Johnson." Firm and entity names are handled the same way as personal names.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>
